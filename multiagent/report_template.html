Research report · multiagent/ · September 2026

Can a placement emerge from many identical, locally informed macros?

Every macro on the chip becomes an agent. All agents run one shared network on what they can see, nudge themselves at the same time, and are judged only on the whole chip's wirelength and overlap. This is what we built, what we measured, and what held up.

Summary

The answers so far

Q1 · Can local agents with one shared brain improve a placement?
Yes, modestly
+7.0% legal HPWL on adaptec1, with the three seeds within 0.2 points of each other (overlap penalty 3).
Q2 · How much does a macro need to see?
It depends on the goal
Seeing only itself is enough on the chip it trained on, because it memorizes the chip. Transfer needs its partners: m0 −1.8% versus m1 +7.5% on bigblue1.
Q3 · Better than optimizing the positions directly?
Mixed
On the same objective, yes: +7.0% against Adam's +2.0%. With each at its best penalty, Adam wins (+16.7%). A 0.5-second swap search (+18.1%) beats both. But agents followed by swaps reach +25–26%, the best adaptec1 result.
Q4 · Does the learned rule help on an unseen chip?
No
The best bigblue1 transfer (+8.6%) is within random jitter's lucky draws (up to +8.3%), and a swap search gets +44%. On ariane133, every transferred policy loses 1–9%.
Swarm rules · Do fish-school dynamics (alignment) help?
No
Alignment takes the gain on adaptec1 from +7% to about 0%. Partners that move together can't shorten the wires between them.
Swap move · Can the agents trade places themselves?
Yes, with an exact local judge
A leaderless swap swarm, where two macros swap when they pick each other, matches the central swap search and beats it on ariane133 (+35.3% vs +33.8%). A judge learned from the agents' view doesn't transfer.
The methodological lesson · three controls
Needed to judge fairly
Random jitter + legalize, a swap search, and a legalizer that works on dense canvases. Without them, the first "+24%" and the bigblue1 transfer looked like wins, and ariane looked unsolvable.
Method

One shared rule, many macros, one score

The design is adaptec1 (ISPD 2005): its first 128 macros on a 224 × 224 grid, core canvas. Every episode starts from the greedy wiremask placement, which places each macro on the legal cell that adds the least wirelength. It's legal and scores 441,257 HPWL. The agents only refine; they never build a placement from scratch.

J(x) = WLₛₘₒₒₜₕ(x) / WL₀ + λ_d · density(x) + λ_o · overlap(x) / macro area

The loss is J averaged over the episode's 33 placements, so a policy that dives and drifts back is penalized. Wirelength is log-sum-exp smoothed (γ = 1 cell) so every connected macro receives a gradient. λ_d = 1 throughout; λ_o, the pairwise overlap penalty, was added in the second sweep.

What a macro sees

ViewInputs per macroFeatures
m0its own position, size and distance to each canvas edge, normalized to the canvas8
m1m0 + its 4 most strongly wired partners: offset from itself, size, connection weight32
m1allm1 + a summary of every wired partner: weighted mean offset (the direction a "move toward your partners" rule would step), weighted spread, connection strength, partner count38
m2m1 + a chip-wide summary (wirelength so far, overlap, episode progress); defined, not studied here35

What every method is compared against

  • Adam on positions (Q3's baseline): the same J minimized directly over the 256 coordinates, with the same bounds and the same legalizer. It's a simple version of DREAMPlace-style analytical placement.
  • Pull rule (untrained control): each step, every macro moves one cell toward the weighted centre of its partners. If the learned policy can't beat this, it learned nothing useful.
  • Swap search (added last): repeatedly apply the single swap of two same-footprint macros that most shortens the wires. It's legal by construction, takes about a second, and has no learning. It makes the one move no continuous method can.
  • Random jitter (control): the greedy start plus 1 cell of Gaussian noise, then the legalizer. It measures how much the legalizer alone can give or take.
  • Legalizer, the same for every method: round to the grid, then, largest macro first, move each overlapping macro to the free spot among its 256 nearest that adds the least wire. The final version also tries a spreading pass first (Finding 5) and keeps whichever legal result is shorter.
  • Metric: legal HPWL after the legalizer, as % improvement over the greedy start. For learned runs, the median of the last 5 checkpoints across 3 seeds, never the best checkpoint (see Fig. 3).
Finding 1

Without an overlap penalty, the agents learn to crowd and the legalizer decides

The first run looked excellent: +24% on adaptec1, above Adam's +21%. Looking closer, the policy was compressing the placement until a third of the macro area overlapped, and handing the legalizer a pile to sort out. Which checkpoint happened to legalize well was close to chance.

Finding 2

Sight, big-then-small steps and the overlap penalty

Three changes, tried separately and combined, with three seeds each. Transfer means applying the adaptec1-trained policy to bigblue1 in one 32-step rollout with no retraining.

Finding 3

What survives an honest measurement

Every run below uses overlap penalty 3. We tested three things: sight (m0 / m1 / m1all); schools (alignment, see the next section); and generalization: noisy starting placements, a random half of macros moving each step, and training on two chips in turn. Each policy is applied without retraining to bigblue1 and to ariane133, a RISC-V core with a different netlist format and die canvas.

Reading it

  • Sight doesn't matter where the policy trained; it matters for transfer. m0 matches m1 on adaptec1 (+6.7% vs +5.6%) because its absolute position lets it memorize the chip. That rule fails on bigblue1 (−1.8%), while m1 transfers at +7.5%.
  • Breaking the memorization helps transfer and costs the home chip. Noisy starts and random-subset moves fall to about 0–2% on adaptec1 but reach +6.6% and +8.6% on bigblue1, above Adam run from scratch there at the same penalty (+4.1%).
  • ariane133 resists every continuous method. Even Adam, optimizing ariane directly, only reaches +0.6%. Finding 5 shows why: first the legalizer, then the kind of move itself.
  • These transfer numbers still need a control. On bigblue1, random jitter plus the legalizer ranges from −8.3% to +8.3% (Finding 6), so the +7–9% transfers are not clearly above chance.
Finding 4

Macros as fish: what swarm research offers, and what it did

Reynolds' boids (1987) produce flocks and fish schools from three local rules, applied by every individual with no leader. Two of the three have existed in chip placement for decades as force-directed placement (Eisenmann & Johannes, 1998; Kraftwerk2, 2008): nets act as springs and density acts as repulsion. Our objective already contains both.

Alignmentmatch neighbours' headingmove with your partners, so clusters travel as schoolsΔᵢ ← (1−a)·Δᵢ + a·mean of partners' Δ, with a ∈ {0.5, 0.9}hurts: adaptec1 +7% → about 0%

Why alignment fails here. Wirelength shrinks only when connected macros move relative to each other, and alignment is designed to make them move together. A school keeps its internal wires the same length. It only helps when a whole cluster should migrate far, which 32 steps of at most one cell rule out, and a big-step schedule plus a penalty can't recover from.

The closest relative in machine learning is the neural cellular automaton (Mordvintsev et al., Growing NCA, 2020). Identical cells run a learned local rule, trained by backprop through the unrolled dynamics, which is our recipe exactly. We borrowed its two training techniques. Random-subset updates gave the best bigblue1 transfer (+8.6%). Varied starting states reduced memorization (+6.6%). Neither rescued ariane133.

Particle-swarm and ant-colony floorplanners share the word "swarm" but not the idea: their agents are candidate solutions, not blocks.

Finding 5

Why ariane133 resisted: first the legalizer, then the move set

ariane133 is 128 identical 17 × 11-cell SRAMs covering 50% of the canvas; adaptec1 covers 29% and bigblue1 3.5%. The original legalizer could only move an overlapping macro into a hole that was already free. On a packed canvas that hole is far away, so one cell of overlap became a ~28-cell jump. Adam shows it clearly: it shortened ariane's raw HPWL by 13%, then the legalizer turned that into −7%.

The fix is the swarm's separation rule, used as a legalizer. Take small gradient steps on the pairwise overlap area alone, so packed neighbours each shift a little instead of one block teleporting, then run the usual repair. Spreading hurts on a sparse canvas, where choosing among free spots is itself an optimizer, so the final legalizer tries both and keeps the shorter legal result. A legal placement passes through untouched.

With a fair legalizer, continuous moves still don't help ariane. Adam reaches +0.6% and the transferred policies −1% to −9%. Yet a plain swap search gets +33.8%. ariane's slack lies in which SRAM sits in which slot. Swapping two blocks with small moves means passing them through each other, which every overlap term forbids. Nudging agents, Adam and fish schools all share that blind spot.

Finding 7

Agents that swap places themselves

Finding 6 found the move every continuous method lacks. This gives it to the agents. Each macro considers trading places with same-footprint macros (so legality is kept by construction). A swap happens only when two macros pick each other, and all agreed swaps in a round happen at once, with no coordinator. A macro judges a swap in one of two ways. The exact judge is the true wirelength change of its own nets: local information, but exact pin geometry. The learned judge is a shared network that reads only the nudging policy's view (m0 / m1 / m1all for both macros) and predicts that change; it's trained on adaptec1.

  • Decentralized swapping works, once conflicts are resolved locally. It's the textbook multi-agent coordination failure, with the textbook fix. The swarm needs fewer parallel rounds (47 on ariane) than the central search's sequential swaps (97).
  • Sight matters again, as range this time. Swaps need to see far: the best partner to trade with is rarely among the nearest 8.
  • Learning when to swap fails the same way learning where to move did: it works partly on the chip it learned from and not elsewhere. A macro can compute its exact swap gain from its own nets, so here the learned judge only adds error.
  • Nudges and swaps combine only on the training chip. Nudges then swaps gives +25.8% on adaptec1 (one policy seed), the best result there. On unseen chips the nudges cost more than they set up.
Limits

What these numbers do not show, and what comes next

  • Three designs, 128 macros, wirelength only. No timing or congestion; none of these benchmarks ships a .lib or .sdc.
  • Adam isn't DREAMPlace. A faithful analytical placer, with electrostatic density and Nesterov steps, is the real bar for Q3.
  • The legalizer still contributes. At penalty 3 the smoothed wirelength falls only about 3% before legalizing; the rest comes from legalizing the few remaining overlaps well. It's the same for every method, and Fig. 8 bounds how much it can give by itself.
  • No critic. Gradients are cut every 8 steps with nothing to bootstrap the future; real SHAC would add a value function.

Next. The swarm that works is the one with an exact local judge and conflict resolution. Learning adds error to a quantity each macro can compute itself. Two directions follow. First, swaps between different-size macros, which need a legal repair and so a learned or searched judge. Second, the nudge + swap swarm as a refinement stage behind a stronger constructive placer, measured against a real DREAMPlace run on more than three designs.
