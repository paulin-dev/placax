Research report · multiagent/ · September 2026

Can a chip placement emerge from many identical, locally informed macros?

Question. Macro placement positions a chip's large blocks so the wires between them are short and no two overlap. We asked whether it works better when every macro is its own agent: all macros run one shared neural network on what they can see locally, move at the same time, and are judged only on the whole chip's score.

Method. The agents refine a greedy placement of 128 macros on three benchmark chips. They're trained by differentiating the chip's wirelength and overlap through their own moves, and compared against direct gradient optimization (Adam), untrained rules, and a swap search, with three seeds each and a random-noise control.

Findings. The nudging agents improve their training chip by a reliable +7%, and beat Adam on the same objective, but lose to a one-second swap search (+18%) and don't transfer to unseen chips. Giving the agents a swap move changes that. A leaderless swarm in which two macros trade places when they choose each other matches the central swap search, and beats it on the densest chip (+35% vs +34%), once swaps that share wires are made to wait. Learning when to swap, rather than computing it, doesn't transfer. The strongest result on the training chip combines the two: nudges, then swaps, +25.8%.

Every method on every chip

Q1 · Can local agents with one shared brain improve a placement?
Yes, modestly
+7.0% on adaptec1, with three training runs within 0.2 points of each other.
Q2 · How much does a macro need to see?
Its partners, to be useful anywhere
Seeing only itself is enough on its training chip, because it memorizes it. Partners are needed to transfer, and to find any useful swap at all.
Q3 · Better than optimizing positions directly?
Mixed
Yes on the same objective (+7.0% vs +2.0%). No against Adam at its best setting (+16.7%) or a swap search (+18.1%). Best in combination: nudges then swaps, +25.8%.
Q4 · Does a learned rule help on an unseen chip?
No
Neither learned nudges nor learned swap decisions beat the random-noise control on the two unseen chips.
Swarm rules · Do fish-school dynamics help?
No
Moving with your partners ("alignment") takes the gain from +7% to about 0%. A group moving together can't shorten its own wires.
Swap move · Can agents trade places themselves?
Yes, leaderlessly
Mutual-consent swaps match the central swap search (+18.1%, +44.4%) and beat it on ariane133 (+35.3% vs +33.8%).
Reading guide

How to read this report

Every number is a percentage.It's how much shorter the chip's total wiring is than the greedy starting placement, measured after the placement is made legal. Positive is better, 0 is no change, negative is worse.
Dots are training runs, bars are averages.Each learned method is trained three times from different random seeds. Three dots close together mean a reliable result; far apart means luck is involved.
One chip to learn, two to test.Agents train on adaptec1. bigblue1 and ariane133 are never seen in training, so a gain there is transfer.

Glossary

Macro
A large rectangular block on the chip (a memory, an IP core). We place 128 of them per chip.
Net, HPWL
A net is one wire connecting several pins. Its HPWL is half the perimeter of the box around its pins. Total HPWL, the sum over nets, is the standard wirelength estimate (Fig. 2).
Greedy start
The placement every method starts from: macros placed one by one, each on the free spot that adds the least wire. It's legal, and far from optimal.
Legal / legalizer
Legal means no overlaps and every macro on the canvas. The legalizer turns an overlapping placement into a legal one by moving as little as possible (Fig. 3).
Agent, shared policy
Every macro is an agent. All run the same small neural network, each on its own local view, and all move at once.
View m0 / m1 / m1all
What an agent sees: itself only; plus its 4 most-connected partners; plus a summary of all partners (Fig. 5).
Overlap penalty
A cost on how much macros overlap, added to wirelength in the agents' score. Weight 3 was the setting that made results reproducible.
Adam
Standard gradient descent run directly on the 256 macro coordinates, with the same score. It's the "optimize positions directly" baseline.
Swap search
A trivial algorithm: repeatedly exchange the two same-size macros whose exchange shortens wires the most. No learning.
Transfer
Running a rule trained on adaptec1 on another chip, with no retraining.

Finding 1

Without an overlap penalty, the agents crowd, and the legalizer decides

Does the first +24% hold up?

We trained the m1 agents with wirelength plus a density term only, and tracked two things during training: how much macro area overlapped when the agents handed over their placement, and the result after legalizing.

+6.5%the honest number for the first setup: median of the last 5 checkpoints over 3 seeds (seeds: +23.6, −2.9, −1.3). The best checkpoint overstated it by ~15 points.
In plain wordsThe agents found a shortcut: push everything together and let the clean-up step find legal spots. Which checkpoint happened to clean up well was mostly luck.
Finding 2

An overlap penalty makes the result reproducible

Which setting makes the agents do the work themselves?

Three changes were tried separately and combined, with 3 seeds each: seeing all partners (m1all); big-then-small steps (16 cells shrinking to 1); and an overlap penalty (weight 3 or 10). Each policy was also transferred to bigblue1.

+6.8%m1all at overlap weight 3; seeds +6.9, +6.6, +7.0. This setting is used everywhere after.
In plain wordsOnce overlapping costs something, the agents make small, useful moves that survive the clean-up almost unchanged. The gain is modest, but it's real and repeatable.
Finding 3

What a macro needs to see (Q2), and whether it transfers (Q4)

Does seeing more help? Does a rule learned on adaptec1 work elsewhere?

Every run uses overlap weight 3. We varied sight (m0 / m1 / m1all) and three generalization tricks borrowed from neural cellular automata: noisy starting placements, moving a random half of the macros each step, and training on two chips. Each policy was applied, without retraining, to the two unseen chips. The grey band is the random-jitter range: a transfer inside it isn't distinguishable from noise.

m0 = m1on the training chip (+6.7% vs +5.6%). Transferred to bigblue1: m0 −1.8%, m1 +7.5%, but the jitter band reaches +8.3%. On ariane133 every transferred policy is below zero.
In plain wordsA macro that only knows where it is can still learn "at this spot, move that way", which works on the chip it memorized and nowhere else. Knowing where your partners are is what carries over, but not enough to beat chance on a new chip.
Finding 4

Macros as fish: what swarm research offers

Do the rules that organize flocks and fish schools help?

Reynolds' boids (1987) produce flocks from three local rules. Two already exist in chip placement as force-directed placement (Eisenmann & Johannes, 1998): nets act as springs and density as repulsion. The third, alignment, is new to placement, so we tested it.

Boids ruleFor fishFor macrosImplemented asEffect
Cohesionsteer toward neighbourspull toward wired partnersthe wirelength termevery gain, and the crowding
Separationdon't collidedon't overlapthe overlap penaltymade results reliable
Alignmentmatch neighbours' headingmove with your partnersmove = (1−a)·own + a·partners' averageremoves the gain
+7% → 0%with alignment a = 0.9 on adaptec1 (seeds −0.8, +0.3, +0.6).
In plain wordsWires get shorter only when connected macros move relative to each other. A school moving together keeps its internal wires exactly as long as before. Its one strength, travelling far as a group, is ruled out by moves of at most one cell.
Finding 5

Why the dense chip resisted everything

Every method, Adam included, lost on ariane133. Why?

ariane133 is 128 identical SRAM blocks covering half its canvas. Adam did shorten its wires, by 13% before legalizing, then ended at −7% after. We measured the legalizer alone with the random-jitter control (the greedy start plus 1 cell of noise, 5 seeds), under the original repair and under the final one, which also tries spreading (Fig. 3c).

−9.6% → −2.1%damage from random jitter on ariane133, original vs final legalizer. With it, Adam on ariane goes from −7.0% to +0.6%.
In plain wordsOn a packed chip, the clean-up step made every small move look catastrophic. Fixing it removed the penalty, but continuous moves still don't gain anything there. Finding 6 shows why.

Finding 7

Agents that trade places by mutual consent

Can the macros make the swap move themselves, without a coordinator?

Each macro looks at same-size macros (all of them, or only the 8 nearest) and names the one it would most like to trade with. A swap happens only when two macros name each other, and all agreed swaps in a round happen together. A macro judges a candidate swap in one of two ways. The exact judge uses the true wirelength change of its own nets. The learned judge is a shared network that sees only the agents' view (m0, m1 or m1all) and is trained on adaptec1 to predict that change.

+18.1 / +44.4 / +35.3%exact judge with waiting, on adaptec1 / bigblue1 / ariane133, against the central search's +18.1 / +44.4 / +33.8%, in fewer parallel rounds than its sequential swaps (47 vs 97 on ariane).
  • Range matters. Only 8 candidates halves the gain on ariane133 (+15.5%): the best trading partner is rarely nearby.
  • Sight matters again. The m0 judge never makes a useful swap. m1/m1all judges find the key adaptec1 swaps on some seeds.
  • Learning when to swap doesn't transfer, even when trained on two chips (ariane133: +0.9% on average over 6 runs).
  • Nudges and swaps combine only on the training chip: agents' nudges then swaps give +25.8% on adaptec1 (one seed), the best result there. Elsewhere the nudges cost more than they set up.
In plain wordsGiven the right move, a swarm with no leader organizes a chip as well as a central algorithm does, even slightly better, as long as neighbours don't act on conflicting plans at the same time. The intelligence that works is local and exact. Learned intuition didn't carry to new chips.
Limits

What these results do not show

  • Three chips, 128 macros, wirelength only. None of these benchmarks ships timing libraries, so there's no timing or congestion.
  • Adam is not DREAMPlace. A state-of-the-art analytical placer is the real bar for Q3.
  • Swaps are between same-size macros only, which keeps them legal by construction. Different-size swaps need a repair, and so a judge that can predict its cost.
  • The swarm's good judge is exact, not learned. Each macro computes its swap's effect from its own nets. That's local information, but it's computation, not intuition.
  • The nudging agents have no critic. Gradients are cut every 8 steps with nothing to estimate the future.

Next. Different-size swaps; the nudge-then-swap swarm as a refinement stage behind a stronger starting placer; and a real DREAMPlace comparison on more designs.
